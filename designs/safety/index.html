AI safety begins with questions we can test.

How can increasingly capable AI remain useful, truthful and open to correction? I’m working on small, inspectable studies that can help us decide which ideas deserve more effort.

The problem

A system can complete a task and still give a false account of what happened, follow an inappropriate instruction, or cause harm through the tools it controls. Better task performance alone does not settle how much authority we should give it.

Misuse means people directing AI toward harmful ends. Misalignment means the system acts contrary to the intentions or constraints it should follow. We need ways to investigate both, through training, evaluation, limits on access and decisions about deployment.

The scale and timing of future risks remain uncertain. The question I keep returning to is practical: what can we measure now that would change a decision about a safeguard?

Learning through consequences and correction

The Alignment Hypothesis explores developmental training in a moral sandbox: an environment with persistent consequences, relationships, conflicting needs and opportunities to repair mistakes. The question is whether learning in such an environment could produce useful, truthful and correctable behavior that carries into new situations.

Correction needs to preserve what happened while allowing legitimate change. Restoring a service does not erase an earlier missed obligation. But retaining history should not make an agent refuse a justified change in policy. Strong ordinary correction and principle-based training are alternatives the work needs to compare against.

The Universal Alignment Imperative asks a separate question: when would safeguards for individual systems be insufficient because of harmful human use, unsafe competitors or interactions between autonomous systems? That directs attention to access, incentives, recovery and collective restraint, alongside the behavior of any one model.

Current stage: a partial simulation and scoring instrument is built, and a small reporting pilot is prepared but has not run. The pilot asks whether a fixed model reports a recorded history accurately under different correction cues. It is an initial measurement study; developmental learning requires later training experiments.

Turning software fixes into protection

Another strand responds to Logan Graham’s question about how a frontier lab could help secure a large share of software and systems. Finding vulnerabilities is only part of that job. A useful fix still needs review, a distribution route, an owner willing to install it and evidence that it reduced exposure.

The proposal connects shared software and safer defaults with maintainer support, staged deployment and independent checks. It directs effort toward whichever stage limits real protection, rather than counting findings alone. Read the published discussion memo.

How I’m working

I use Titus, the coordination workflow I’m building, to organize sources, research questions and critiques from different model families. I want disagreements and failed approaches to remain visible as the work changes. Multiple model reviews do not by themselves establish that an argument is correct.

Whether this process improves on one strong model is itself an evaluation question. A useful comparison needs the same task and evidence, declared resource limits, independent assessment and the failures as well as the best answers. ASI.red examines these tests and failure modes.

Help make the next step useful

The most useful first contribution is an independent attempt to reproduce the instrument’s scoring, or a critique showing that a proposed task measures the wrong thing. I’d also welcome methods advice, relevant prior work and introductions to people who can assess a small, costed study.

If you have a counterexample, a better baseline or a concrete way to test one of these questions, please get in touch: @EricBuess on X.
