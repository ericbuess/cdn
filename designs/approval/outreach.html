Proposed first support period

Ask initially for one appropriate seat or a capped research allocation, reviewed after 30 days. Expand only if the workload and evidence justify it. These are proposed deliverables, not completed results or a promise that a particular access program will permit them:

  1. A public methods package for one independently reproducible measurement instrument, with its tests and unresolved validity questions.
  2. A small model pilot only after the execution and measurement gates pass; otherwise publish the reasons it did not run. Report all attempts and missing results.
  3. A defined comparison of one strong model, repeated independent samples and different-provider critique, with matched resource accounting where possible. Distinguish model quality from harness effects and do not use model agreement as ground truth.
  4. A short progress and usage report linking artifacts, accepted/rejected changes and actual contributor metadata. Separate observed token counts from estimates or rounded account-level usage; unknown remains unknown.

Publication is a transparency commitment, not a promise of favorable coverage. Any material support should be disclosed. No private board contents, household transcripts, credentials, confidential program participation or hidden model reasoning would be included.

Article draft: What would count as making a system safer?

I have been thinking about a question Logan Graham raised: how could a frontier lab help secure a large fraction of the software and systems people depend on?

The question sits inside a difficult conversation about the pace of AI development. I do not think software hardening settles the questions about alignment or what capabilities should be released. But while those questions remain open, it seems useful to ask what practical protection we could build and how we would know it worked.

Logan also described a more immediate possibility: an owner submits a system and gets rapid, tested improvements without disruption. I find that direction helpful because it connects a technical result to something an operator could actually use.

There is a lot between finding a bug and protecting a system. A report has to be checked. A maintainer may need time to understand it. A fix can break something else. The people operating a system have their own requirements, and an available update may still never reach the affected machines.

Important work on these problems is already underway. I do not want to present a familiar idea as a discovery, or assume I understand the constraints better than the people handling them. My proposal is to select one supported deployment class and make the whole path visible: inventory, permission, change, testing, approval, deployment, verification and recovery.

For familiar upgrades or configuration changes that an operator has approved in advance, an hour might be a useful target to test. For a novel patch or a change with serious consequences, it would be misleading to promise that. I would separate those paths and count the unsuccessful attempts as carefully as the successful ones.

The first program could be small: consenting partners, a limited set of replayable cases, some carefully controlled deployments and independent review. Before starting, the participants would agree on what improvement would be useful and what kinds of failure would stop the trial. At the end, the result might justify expansion. It might also show that this approach needs revision or that another bottleneck matters more.

I worked on this proposal with Titus, the agent coordination workflow I am developing. Titus helps organize research and critique from models built by different providers. I find their disagreements useful, but I am trying to be careful about what that means.

Several reviews caught consequential problems in the early draft. Some reviews also introduced new claims that were not supported. Agreement between models did not resolve that; returning to the sources did. The final document includes a short account of who contributed and what could not be verified. An early Claude attempt did not return a final answer and is not counted. Later Opus and Fable reviews completed and led to further corrections; the record also retains their limits.

I would like to test this process against a strong single-model workflow at comparable resources. Does it catch more important errors? Does it produce repairs that survive independent checks? When does another round help, and when does it simply add cost or confidence? Open-weight frontier participation is still to be added. I do not yet have evidence that the process is generally better.

This connects to my broader Alignment Hypothesis work, which asks about developmental environments with persistent consequences, relationships and correction. The immediate research is much narrower than that ambition: developing a measurement instrument and preparing a small reporting/correction pilot. It has not demonstrated developmental learning or an alignment solution.

I am also exploring whether people could contribute bounded research and validation tasks through a concept I call ASI.contractors. Before expanding that idea, we would need clear authority, inspectable evidence and a way to avoid rewarding confident reports that do not correspond to useful work. It is an early design question, not an operating marketplace.

For now, I hope the brief is useful as something concrete to examine. I would appreciate hearing where it duplicates existing work, where its assumptions fail and what a first test would need to show. If parts do not hold up, I want to learn that while they are still easy to change.
