I’m focusing my work on AI safety: how increasingly capable systems can remain open to correction, and how we can test that carefully.

I build with Titus, my evolving system for bringing frontier models and their tools together to research, debate and make things. The Alignment Hypothesis is one part of that work. Going screenless is another: spending less time navigating apps, leaving more room for my family and the people in front of me, and teaching others how to do the same.

Alignment starts
with me.

My interest in AI alignment grows out of my own practice of moral alignment.

Prayer, moral reflection and practicing my faith help me continually reorient toward an ever-clearer understanding of what is good. I use tools such as AI to reflect on my commitments, evaluate the important areas of my life, and notice where my actions need to change.

Paul and Titus walking in the same spirit and the same steps, and the image of iron sharpening iron, shape how I think about mutual correction. That personal work is central to why I pursue AI alignment and safety, alongside my concern for our five children and the wider world. I want to keep learning, remain open to correction, and build tools that help others do the same.

The system behind the work

Titus: keeping the
work connected.

Different models and tools, with a shared record of what matters.

Titus is the system I’m building to help different AI tools carry work forward together. I use it to organize research, compare approaches and keep track of the sources, decisions and unfinished work behind a project.

The idea is simple: a useful collaborator should remember what matters, make room for disagreement and change course when the evidence calls for it. I want a correction to survive the next conversation, even when the model or tool changes.

There are working pieces and source-linked reviews to inspect, with more integration and testing ahead. I’m especially interested in finding out when several models contribute something useful beyond one strong model working alone. Go Screenless is the voice experience I’m developing alongside this work; Titus is intended to support typed conversations too.

I want to be able to talk while living my life, hear useful responses, and stay present for the people around me.

From my life, into the work.

I’ve used a Limitless pendant with my earlier Titus app to capture speech and transcribe it on my phone. It helped me direct work by voice while spending less time at a keyboard.

I’m rebuilding that experience around Go Screenless, with the Apple Watch as a starting point so more people can try it without buying another device. I also have an Omi pendant to explore. The new Watch-to-agent conversation loop is still being connected.

A voice, with a team behind it.

Titus brings model contributions into shared, source-linked research and review. I’m working toward a single place to give direction and hear progress, even as the tools and models change.

Optional ongoing capture with local phone transcription is another direction I’m exploring. It is separate from deliberately sending a request to an agent.

How I work.

“Iron sharpens iron” is an aspiration for mutual correction. Whether different models actually improve the work is something to measure.

  1. Draft with multiple models.

    I’ve used frontier models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI and Meta for research and critique through their native tools. I preserve their sources, contributions and disagreements; open-weight models are a planned addition.

  2. Inspect and test the output.

    Work such as Together AI’s Mixture of Agents helps motivate the question. I’m preparing comparisons against one strong model, independent samples and different review roles. More voices are not automatically better; these comparisons have not yet established a benefit in my system.

  3. Keep the decision human.

    I keep responsibility for consequential decisions and a record of what changed and why. Reliable recurring work across every connected tool is still being built; agreement between models is not proof that an answer is right.
