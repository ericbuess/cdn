(b) Speculation section — category 6

Every line here is speculation. None of it is a finding, none is sourced, and it should be labeled as speculation anywhere it appears in the article.

SPECULATION (editor). The Alignment Hypothesis characteristics likeliest to survive scrutiny are (1) persistent consequences — actions a reset cannot undo; (2) real counterparties whose interests differ from the model's; (3) legitimate correction — being told no by someone with standing, repeatedly, over time. The characteristic likeliest not to survive is the assumption that these must be human relationships.

SPECULATION (editor). A legitimate firewall differs from a toy one in that the boundary is enforced by something the model cannot represent as part of its task — physical separation, an independent party, a cryptographic gate held by someone else — rather than by a rule living inside the model's own context. A toy sandbox is one where the constraint and the optimizer share a head.

SPECULATION (grok). Legitimate: shared state others inhabit, lasting costs, correction that can be refused, and logs that are not secretly the reward. Toy: reset-anytime, no third-party welfare, grader equals trainer. "Grader equals trainer" is the crispest test the round produced.

SPECULATION (opus-thinking). Persistent consequences survives because it removes the reset that makes toy sandboxes cheap to game. Legitimate correction survives conditional on the corrector holding real authority the model cannot route around. Real relationships survives only partially — it reduces to a claim about counterparty persistence and reputation, not about felt attachment.

SPECULATION (editor, on inevitability). A foundation world-model looks close to inevitable on capability grounds alone, because agents need to predict consequences. A shared moral-training environment does not. The first is a competitive advantage; the second is a public good, and public goods do not get built by default. If a moral-training environment happens it will be because someone funds it as infrastructure.

SPECULATION (grok, dissenting on inevitability). A foundation world-model as an inevitable path is speculation; treat persistence and memory as testable modifiers instead. grok notes the project's own internal reconciliation already rejected a required foundation world-model absent evidence. Recorded dissent: codex agrees such an environment may become useful but says inevitability is unsupported; sol says the same. The round does not support calling it inevitable.

SPECULATION (editor). The likeliest path is that persistent multi-agent worlds get built for capability reasons, with alignment as a side-constraint — so the alignment-relevant environment arrives as someone else's byproduct, designed for the wrong objective.

SPECULATION (opus-thinking). A moral-training environment is likelier to arrive as an evaluation harness than as a pretraining substrate.

(c) Quality on-ramp — merged

Where it is right. Ideas are not the bottleneck in alignment work. Formalization and reproduction are, and both are what scaffolding automates (editor, opus-thinking). Most outside contributions fail on form, not on insight, and form is mechanizable (editor, opus-thinking, grok). There are far more people capable of a good question than of a good paper. Templates, preregistration, frozen questions, claim IDs and reproducible harnesses convert enthusiasm into checkable, fail-able artifacts (all seven). "Everyone has a role" is true for replication, red-teaming, data curation, documentation, domain review and finding holes in public arguments (sol, codex, grok, muse).

Where it is naive. Scaffolding raises the floor and also raises the volume, and volume is exactly what reviewers fear. Better-formatted wrong work is harder to dismiss, not easier (editor). Reviewers and maintainers are the scarce resource, so scaffolding that generates more submissions without generating more evaluation capacity makes the problem worse (opus-thinking, grok). The global-RL metaphor fails on its central term: RL needs a reward signal, and safety research has no fast ground truth — that is the whole problem (editor, opus-thinking, codex, muse all reached this independently; it is the round's most unanimous criticism). Access to models is not expertise (codex). Templates do not create tacit experimental skill, and more samples do not fix a worse estimand (grok). "Everyone has a role" is false at the sharp end, and infohazard handling is a real reason for closed doors (opus-thinking, gemini).

How someone on the periphery actually plugs in. The round's most reusable finding is that the binding shortage is reviewer, replicator and installer capacity rather than ideas or goodwill — the coordinated-disclosure work says in its own words that human triage is the rate-limiting step and that a patch merged upstream is not a patch installed. That maps to concrete, unglamorous work that does not require being inside a lab: reproduce a published result and report what happened, including when nothing happened; take a public eval harness and run it against a model nobody has run it against; help an open-source maintainer actually apply a disclosed fix; translate a paper's method into a runnable artifact; write a clean negative result into a public registry; stress-test a public argument, including this article's, and say precisely where it breaks. Each of these has cheap ground truth, which is why they can absorb new people without taxing the scarce reviewers. None of them requires anyone's permission, and none of them is a product — they are existing open programs across several labs, independent evaluators and universities, and the way in is to pick one and do the boring version of it well. The honest caveat: this is the on-ramp, not the summit, and the sharp end still needs years of training and, for dual-use work, closed doors.

Minimum scaffolding — merged, in order. (1) A claim template: one falsifiable statement, the method, the prediction, and what would refute it. (2) A reproducible artifact requirement — code, seed and environment hash, or it does not count. (3) Automated novelty and prior-work checking before a human sees it. (4) A preregistered failure criterion, declared before the run. (5) Independent replication by a different model family. (6) A public registry of negative results, so the same dead end is not walked twice. (7) A triage layer routing to human review only what passed the mechanical gates, with published acceptance rates. (8) A disclosure path for dual-use findings, and a hard ban on live third-party systems and working exploits (grok). (9) A gate preventing results from directly granting credentials, money or deployment authority (codex). Start with replication and red-teaming, where ground truth is cheap, before opening the theory lane (editor).
