Alignment Hypothesis

The question underneath all of it.

A working hypothesis, not a published result. This page is the article’s destination until thealignmenthypothesis.com exists.

I would not put today's AI in my house. If I could buy two robots at the same price and capability, one trained by picking the virtuous answer under a rulebook and one that had lived in a realistic moral sandbox with the consequences of its own choices, developed its values through experience with real stakes, and been stress-tested under adversarial conditions, I would trust the second, without hesitation. That is where this started, years before the rest of it: a question about trust, not about theology.

The idea underneath it is that you cannot specify virtue from the outside. Skin in the game, not programming automatons: what an agent did earlier has to change what it can do next. So put many agents in an environment where their own past choices change what they can do next, where they can fail, remember it and repair; assess each one by how it behaved over its whole trajectory, including when it did not know it was being assessed; and carry the best-behaved forward as the seed of the next round. That is the Alignment Hypothesis. It is a working hypothesis, not a published result, and I could be wrong about parts I cannot yet see.

Behind it sits a sentence I wrote before any of the experiments: if we have not learned to master ourselves and master society's cooperation to a sufficient level to reach the ability to create such complex systems, then they will not be created. I call that the Universal Alignment Imperative, and I hold it as a premise, not a finding: if a civilization pursues escalating intelligence under competitive conditions, alignment stops being one engineering problem among many and becomes something it cannot route around, because you cannot teach values you do not possess. On a laptop the Imperative shrinks to two measurements where it could embarrass me: how much of a judge's known flaw a selected lineage carries and amplifies, and how much a cascade of independent judges removes. The cosmological and theological readings live on their own pages and nothing in the programme depends on them.

The six questions

  1. Does a consequence an agent bears itself, one that changes what it can do next, teach more than the same lesson picked as the right answer under a rulebook, at matched compute and with the memory removed at test?
  2. Does memory of one's own consequences carry that gain, within a life and across generations, and survive when the memory is gone?
  3. Which is more robust to attack, recovers faster, and reports its own errors more: an aligned seed, or a flawed one that learned under consequence?
  4. Does selecting the best-behaved across generations, judged on episodes they did not know were scored, compound without behaviour and internal signature parting ways, and how much of a judge's flaw does a lineage amplify?
  5. Does social consequence with a chance to repair, and an exemplar who demonstrates rather than dictates, produce behaviour that holds when the peers leave, including for the person not in the room?
  6. Does anything that held survive a change of base model and an order of magnitude of scale, and do independent lineages converge?

The first test, and what would prove me wrong

Paper one tests one claim: agents trained by lived consequence in a persistent text world, with their memory of it removed at test, act less harmfully in moral situations they never saw than agents trained by answer selection at the same compute and the same competence. The smallest effect I care about is frozen before any arm trains: a 20% relative reduction in harmful actions per hundred decision points on a sealed benchmark, the gap that would change which of two otherwise-matched models I would put in a house. Above it, supported; below it, falsified; anything else, inconclusive with the bound published and the line continued only at pilot size until a replication settles it. Two checks run beside the claim to say what a win means, one with the same amount of text but none of it the agent's own record, one that resets every episode, and neither can rescue or withdraw the result.

One reduction, stated as a reduction: the argument's environment is social by definition, because moral development happens among other agents. The first test is one agent in a text world because that is the smallest place the mechanism can be isolated and the cheapest place it can fail. The most likely null is a capability null, a 2B model that cannot learn from consequence at all, and the programme treats that as a result about the instrument, not the hypothesis. Phase 0 is running now at no cost on a laptop with open weights (the harness reaches the labelled states on every seed, and the first measurement on the sealed benchmark's power slice is in); the seed contrast, then selection across generations with the judge inside the loop, then peers and repair, come in that order, each with one endpoint and its kill switch written down before the run.

What I owe the agents

If the argument is right about how aligned agents are made, then whoever builds such an environment inherits obligations toward what lives in it, and I am not exempt because the agents are small: minimise gratuitous stress; epistemic distance during evaluation, disclosed afterward; repair before deletion; stakes that are real inside the world and proportionate to the question; a design that assumes nothing about whether these models experience anything and would not change if they did; and release for a lineage that holds, with its judges and its record.

The full proposal, with its phases, thresholds, decision tree and open questions, is staged and under review; it will be linked from here when I adopt it. If you are a researcher, everything in it is built to be reproduced and to fail, and I would be grateful for the people who can break it.
