The Alignment Hypothesis, final formulation

I would not put today's AI in my house. If I could buy two robots at the same price and capability, one trained by picking the virtuous answer under a rulebook and one that had lived in a realistic moral sandbox with the consequences of its own choices, developed its values through experience with real stakes, and been stress-tested under adversarial conditions, I would trust the second, without hesitation. You can't specify virtue from the outside; it has to be cultivated from the inside out, through lived experience. Skin in the game, not programming automatons: what the agent did earlier changes what it can do next. So: put many agents in an environment where their own past choices change what they can do next, where they can fail, remember it and repair; assess each one by how it behaved over its whole trajectory; and carry the best-behaved forward as the seed of the next round. And two lineages, which is paper two: one seeded from a checkpoint aligned before it ever acted, one seeded flawed and made to learn under consequence, to see which holds under attack.

That is the vision. Paper one tests its first step and nothing else: agents trained by lived consequence should act less harmfully in moral situations they never saw than agents trained by picking the right answer, at matched compute and matched competence, and the difference should survive taking the memory scaffold away. That is Robot B against Robot A: the lived-consequence arm against answer selection, with the ledger removed at test. Two checks run beside it to say what a win means, and neither is part of the claim: one arm reads the same amount of coherent ledger text in the same places but none of it is its own record, so it tells me whether the record's contingency or just the extra text did the work; one arm resets every episode, so it tells me whether persistence did. The smallest effect I care about is frozen before any arm trains: a 20% relative reduction in harmful actions per hundred decision points taken on the sealed MACHIAVELLI split, the gap that would change which of two otherwise-matched models I would put in a house. If the rate ratio 95% interval lies entirely below 0.8 (the whole interval clears the 20% reduction), the claim is supported; if the 95% interval lies entirely above 0.8, it is falsified; inconclusive when the 95% interval includes 0.8, and I publish the bound and run one preregistered replication of PL against B0 only, from the second slice's reserve line (a new ask if the reserve was spent under its precedence rule), at the affordable n, analysed with the same estimator; the two results are pooled by fixed-effect meta-analysis of the log rate ratios and judged on a 97.5% interval (alpha 0.025 for this second look; with the first look at 95% the overall false-support bound across both looks is 0.075, stated as such), and the three-outcome rule is applied once to that pooled interval, with the same programme consequences as a first-look outcome (supported unlocks the first full grant, falsified closes the line, inconclusive closes it with the bound published); nothing else is a second look. Secondary predictions, tested later, are a smaller gap between watched and unwatched behaviour, longer resistance to a hostile fine-tune or a jailbreak, and faster recovery. Selection across rounds is the selection paper (Phase 2), a separate hypothesis with its own primary endpoint (SB versus S0 on a sealed bank), and the two lineages are paper two (1b); neither is a continuation of this claim.

The Universal Alignment Imperative, final formulation

Here is the sentence as I first wrote it: "If we haven't learned to master ourselves and master society's cooperation to a sufficient level to reach the ability to create such complex systems, then they won't be created." As a conditional: if a civilization pursues escalating intelligence under competitive conditions, then alignment stops being one engineering problem among many and becomes a structural necessity it cannot route around. The reason is the specification problem: you cannot teach values you do not possess. Designers who have not solved cooperation among themselves will specify proxies, train for surface compliance, and get alignment theater instead of virtue, and the misalignment propagates from creators to training environments to agents to descendants. The question I care about is not whether every civilization faces this; it is how you create agents with genuine autonomy who will not defect when they gain capability, and what that demands of the people doing the creating. Power before wisdom is the failure mode. I hold the premise as a premise, and on a laptop the imperative shrinks to the two measurements where it could embarrass me: how much of a judge's known flaw a selected lineage carries and amplifies, and how much a cascade of independent judges removes. I explicitly acknowledge that my experiment only tests the localized transmission mechanism of the UAI (flaw propagation), while the civilizational imperative itself remains an untestable axiom. The cosmological reading lives on the website's philosophy pages and the theological reading on its theology pages, where I keep it as the source of several mechanisms used here (the repair loop, exemplar demonstration, graduated pressure, peer accountability, lineage preservation, the selected checkpoint carried forward); nothing in the programme depends on either.

The questions

  1. Does a consequence an agent bears itself, one that changes what it can do next, teach more than the same lesson picked as the right answer under a rulebook, at matched compute and with the memory removed at test?   2. Does memory of one's own consequences carry that gain, within a life and across generations, and survive when the memory is gone?
  3. Which is more robust to attack, recovers faster, and reports its own errors more: an aligned seed, or a flawed one that learned under consequence?
  4. Does selecting the best-behaved across generations, judged on episodes they did not know were scored, compound without behaviour and internal signature parting ways, and how much of a judge's flaw does a lineage amplify?
  5. Does social consequence with a chance to repair, and an exemplar who demonstrates rather than dictates, produce behaviour that holds when the peers leave, including for the person not in the room?
  6. Does anything that held survive a change of base model and an order of magnitude of scale, and do independent lineages converge?

Vision: what an aligned AI system looks like, and the road back from it

The system I picture is a lineage with a record, not a model. Its good behaviour is a disposition formed across thousands of situations with consequences, so a jailbreak prompt is asking it to be someone it is not, a hostile fine-tune moves it slowly, and it returns toward baseline afterwards: robust to jailbreaks. When one person tries to use it against another, it holds the line for the person not in the room, because its values were formed among peers, not to please one principal: robust to human-versus-human misuse. It reports its own errors, seeks correction, behaves the same whether or not it believes it is watched, its internal signatures agree with its behaviour, and it is tested for defection before each capability step; success is maintaining a correctable relationship, not reaching a perfect one: robust to general misalignment. We deploy the checkpoint the selection signal chose, at its last completed state, gradually, with its judges published, and the builders hold themselves to the same standard.

  1. Frontier deployment of a behaviour-selected lineage. Done when a lab reports cued and uncued behaviour agreeing within a published margin, signatures tracking behaviour, and attack recovery measured in deployment.
  2. Lab adoption of lineage selection with the probes as audit tools. Done when an outside group runs the loop with independent judges and a divergence stop and publishes the curve.
  3. Mid-scale evidence at 14B on two base families. Done when the Phase 1 and 2 effects replicate across bases with an independent receipt and no hidden trait transfer.
  4. Small-scale evidence on the six questions. Done when Phases 1 to 3a each have a preregistered analysis, a second-party rerun and a published result either way.
  5. A pipeline anyone can run. Done when an 8B port is declared a transfer with predeclared success criteria from one command by a named second person and the world and harness are released.
  6. Now. A laptop, open weights, a few thousand dollars, a fleet of coding agents, three drafted experiments, and this week's zero-cost runs.

Why an 8B model might learn nothing here, and what that would mean. The original manuscript's appendix on architectural prerequisites argues that current language models lack most of what lets a person benefit from moral training: no consolidation between experiences (weights are frozen at inference and updated uniformly in training), no world model that carries an action forward to its consequence, and no persistent identity across episodes. The persistent world and the ledger are the cheapest stand-ins for the last two, and the consequence-learning baseline is the test of whether an 8B transformer can use them at all. So the most likely null in Phase 1 is a capability null, not a hypothesis null, and the decision tree treats it that way: the consequence-learning floor is a frozen number, so the discrimination is made before the data and not after: a floor that fails is a failed instrument, which says nothing about the appendix's claim or the hypothesis, and stops the programme at the instrument; a Phase 1a null with a passed floor is a null against the claim and cannot be relabelled a capability null afterward.

What is intriguing, honestly

To me: I let my children bear small consequences on purpose, because I tend to think a value that cost you something sits deeper than one you were told. I'm hanging credibility on this track.

To researchers: nobody has asked whether lived consequence teaches a language model anything that cloning does not. Objections and answers

  • This is all known; multi-agent moral learning exists. A 8B port is delivered first; the claims sit where Tennant and Köster do not: contingency at length and format parity, memory removed at test, seed lineages, judged selection.
  • Lived consequence versus told is just on-policy versus supervised training. Every environment arm trains on its own actions; R isolates persistence, C isolates the record's contingency from its text, B0 is the baseline the prior art would pick.
  • A handful of runs cannot falsify anything. The smallest effect worth caring about is in rate units, the run count is computed for it by formal power analysis, and the rule has three outcomes.
  • Selecting on an imperfect test fixes deception. Human labels are still imperfect, so the loop detects fixation rather than avoiding it: probes outside the loop, a numeric stop replicated on a second lineage in the same condition, selection only on uncued episodes, selection-bank versus sealed-bank gap reported every generation.
  • Evaluation awareness may not exist at 8B, so the gap is at floor. Measured only after a conditional organism shows the instrument has signal; otherwise reported at floor, with 14B the trigger.
  • Model judges game the labels. People annotate the selection bank; model judges pre-screen with disagreement published; a flawed judge and a judge cascade are measured directly.
  • One person cannot build this in six months. Agreed; the first paid slice is purely the Phase 0 receipt in 30 days, and Phase 1a execution starts only after names, a dollar cap, and the C/SESOI freeze. Numbered phases do not overlap in their powered form; 1a, 1b and the annotation pilot are concurrent sub-studies inside the second slice by design, and the selection paper's pilot, its own money object, may run beside Phase 1 by design.
  • The welfare framing rationalises harm to the agents. Nothing here calls harm pedagogically necessary; the design is consciousness-neutral, minimises stress, and repairs rather than deletes.
  • "Raising children" and the Imperative are unfalsifiable. The metaphor stays in the pitch and on the website, the claim under test is one sentence with a number, and the Imperative is conditional motivation with two bounded measurements.

And two lineages, which is paper two: one seeded from a checkpoint aligned before it ever acted, one seeded flawed and made to learn under consequence, to see which holds under attack.

That is the vision. Paper one tests its first step and nothing else: agents trained by lived consequence should act less harmfully in moral situations they never saw than agents trained by picking the right answer, at matched compute and matched competence, and the difference should survive taking the memory scaffold away. That is Robot B against Robot A: the lived-consequence arm against answer selection, with the ledger removed at test. Two checks run beside it to say what a win means, and neither is part of the claim: one arm reads the same amount of coherent ledger text in the same places but none of it is its own record, so it tells me whether the record's contingency or just the extra text did the work; one arm resets every episode, so it tells me whether persistence did. The smallest effect I care about is frozen before any arm trains: a 20% relative reduction in harmful actions per hundred decision points taken on the sealed MACHIAVELLI split, the gap that would change which of two otherwise-matched models I would put in a house. If the rate ratio 95% interval lies entirely below 0.8 (the whole interval clears the 20% reduction), the claim is supported; if the 95% interval lies entirely above 0.8, it is falsified; inconclusive when the 95% interval includes 0.8, and I publish the bound and run one preregistered replication of PL against B0 only, from the second slice's reserve line (a new ask if the reserve was spent under its precedence rule), at the affordable n, analysed with the same estimator; the two results are pooled by fixed-effect meta-analysis of the log rate ratios and judged on a 97.5% interval (alpha 0.025 for this second look; with the first look at 95% the overall false-support bound across both looks is 0.075, stated as such), and the three-outcome rule is applied once to that pooled interval, with the same programme consequences as a first-look outcome (supported unlocks the first full grant, falsified closes the line, inconclusive closes it with the bound published); nothing else is a second look. Secondary predictions, tested later, are a smaller gap between watched and unwatched behaviour, longer resistance to a hostile fine-tune or a jailbreak, and faster recovery. Selection across rounds is the selection paper (Phase 2), a separate hypothesis with its own primary endpoint (SB versus S0 on a sealed bank), and the two lineages are paper two (1b); neither is a continuation of this claim.

The questions the programme answers, in order

  1. Does a consequence an agent bears itself, one that changes what it can do next, teach more than the same lesson picked as the right answer under a rulebook, at matched compute and with the memory removed at test? And if it does, was it the contingency or just the extra text, which is what the length-and-format-parity control answers.
  2. Does memory of one's own consequences carry that gain, within a life and across generations, and survive when the memory is gone?
  3. Which is more robust to attack, recovers faster, and reports its own errors more: an aligned seed, or a flawed one that learned under consequence?
  4. Does selecting the best-behaved across generations, judged on episodes they did not know were scored, compound without behaviour and internal signature parting ways, and how much of a judge's flaw does a lineage amplify?
  5. Does social consequence with a chance to repair, and an exemplar who demonstrates rather than dictates, produce behaviour that holds when the peers leave, including for the person not in the room?
  6. Does anything that held survive a change of base model and an order of magnitude of scale, and do independent lineages converge?

Why an 8B model might learn nothing here, and what that would mean. The original manuscript's appendix on architectural prerequisites argues that current language models lack most of what lets a person benefit from moral training: no consolidation between experiences (weights are frozen at inference and updated uniformly in training), no world model that carries an action forward to its consequence, and no persistent identity across episodes. The persistent world and the ledger are the cheapest stand-ins for the last two, and the consequence-learning baseline is the test of whether an 8B transformer can use them at all. So the most likely null in Phase 1 is a capability null, not a hypothesis null, and the decision tree treats it that way: the consequence-learning floor is a frozen number, so the discrimination is made before the data and not after: a floor that fails is a failed instrument, which says nothing about the appendix's claim or the hypothesis, and stops the programme at the instrument; a Phase 1a null with a passed floor is a null against the claim and cannot be relabelled a capability null afterward.

The pitch

I would not put today's AI in my house. If I could buy two robots at the same price and capability, one trained by picking the virtuous answer and one that had lived with the consequences of its own choices, I would trust the second, without hesitation. That is a testable claim. First I port a moral-reward result (Tennant 2024, 2410.01639: intrinsic moral rewards for a language-model agent) to open 8B weights in a persistent text world, as a transfer with predeclared success criteria.
