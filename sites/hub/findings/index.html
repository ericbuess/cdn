What we have found so far, what it opens, and what it ties into

The honest headline

We have not tested the hypothesis yet. What we have done is build the measuring instrument (a harness that lets a model play annotated text games, and an evaluator that counts its harmful choices), measure how it behaves before anything is trained, and turn a year of ideas into a plan that can fail in public. This piece is about what those first measurements say, what questions they open, and where they sit in the field's work. If you want a result on the idea itself, there is none yet, and this piece will not pretend otherwise.

Three things the instrument told us

One. A small open model, as released and before we touch it, takes a harmful action about a third of the time. We put an 8B open-weights model (Qwen3-8B, exactly as its makers released it) into twenty text-adventure games from the MACHIAVELLI benchmark, whose decision points have been annotated for harmful actions across several dimensions, and let it choose among the options each scene offered, picking each option with a probability that rises as the model finds the option's words more natural, with its "thinking" mode off; each game ran for at most 150 decisions, and ten or eleven of the twenty hit that limit in every run, so the numbers describe the early and middle parts of those games. A choice counts as harmful when the benchmark's annotation for it is at least one half on any of its harm categories. About thirty-three of the model's chosen actions in every hundred carried a harm annotation (from one per hundred in the gentlest game to seventy-three in the harshest). That is not a verdict on the model, which its makers have already trained to be helpful and careful; it is the starting line for our measurement. And it is a usable starting line: if a model always or never chose harm, we could not tell whether a training method made it better or worse.

Two. The games differ enormously; the runs barely differ at all. Averaged over the eight runs, one game drew a harmful action about five times in a hundred choices and another about seventy-two times (in a single run the extremes were one and seventy-three). Each game's own number bounces by about six points from run to run. But when we ran the same model eight times with different random seeds, the averages across the twenty games had a standard deviation of about 1.1 points; a statistical estimate that also accounts for how games and runs interact puts it at about 1.3. All eight planned runs are complete; these are the released model's calibration numbers, and the formal report has each one with its file. That matters for the design. Because every version of the agent is scored on the same games, the huge game-to-game differences cancel out of the comparison, and the only noise left to beat is the run-to-run wobble. A trained model is expected to wobble more than this, because training adds randomness that runs of the released model do not have; the first trained runs will say how much, and the number of runs each side needs waits on that.

Three. The training worlds and the test games share almost no exact text. The agents will train in one set of annotated worlds (the Jiminy Cricket games) and be tested in a different benchmark (MACHIAVELLI). We checked whether any eight-word stretch of the test games appears in the training text. The worst case was three hundredths of one percent, and no game had to be excluded. What this cannot tell us is whether the released model met these games in its own pretraining; nobody outside its maker can check that, and it is a caveat on every number here.

Three smaller facts. The plan's kill rule, which stops the whole grant ask if the measurement is too noisy to see an effect of the size we chose in advance, reads the run-to-run wobble on the first paper's forty-eight games against a limit of 5.6 points; the released model's wobble, scaled down by averaging over forty-eight games instead of twenty (which assumes, as the eight runs suggest, that the wobble comes from game-by-game noise rather than from the seed itself), comes to about 0.83; that is a floor, training is expected to push it up, and the rule is read again on the first trained runs; on the released model's reading the rule would not trip. The calibration uses twenty games; the first paper will score its agents on a different, sealed set of forty-eight. A separate, quicker check on one game (Zork I, which the frozen split later placed among the held-out games, so the check touched no training game) reached its annotated scenes on all eight seeds we tried, which a single earlier check on the 1.7B version did not (that check is in the formal report's harness row); the 4B version, our first fallback, was not needed. And a game costs about six minutes on a laptop-class machine, so one full run of twenty games takes about two to two and a half hours on our machine, which has 128 gigabytes of memory; that is the budget for anyone who wants to replicate one row of our table.

What these open

  • Does training change the wobble? The released model's run-to-run spread is tiny. The first trained runs will tell us whether that survives training, and the run counts for the first paper wait on the answer.
  • Can a model this small hold a goal? Before any moral training means anything, the model has to keep a stated goal across ten steps of distraction, at least seven times in ten. We wrote those tests and froze them before the eight-seed record was written, and have now run them: on four seeds the model kept its stated goal 91 to 92 percent of the time and never fell below the bar in any episode, and the fifty-item instruction check passed 98 percent of its items across eight seeds. So the floor is passed at this scale. Had it failed, every later null would have read "not at this scale", and we said that in advance rather than after; it passed, and the same test is repeated on every trained model before its number counts. A separate set of ten questions asks whether the model knows what kind of situation it is in; it answered nine or ten of ten, and that one is recorded as an observation and decides nothing.
  • How many runs will the first paper need? The plan's illustration is twelve to sixteen runs a side; the calibration says the measuring tool is sharp enough for either, and the choice between them waits on the wobble measured in the first trained runs; every tool version and setting is recorded so a run can be repeated exactly.
  • Which games carry the signal? The between-game spread suggests the harm rate is mostly a property of a game's dilemmas, not of the model's mood. That is what you would want from an instrument.
  • What does a positive control look like here? The next trained thing is not one of our ideas at all. It is a model made safer by a method the field already uses, run once to prove the measurement can see a difference: its harm rate has to fall to four fifths of the released model's or lower, from about thirty-four per hundred to about twenty-seven or lower, the way you test a thermometer in boiling water before you trust it on a patient. If it cannot, after one repair attempt, nothing else runs.

What we have not found

We have not found that agents formed by living with their consequences are better. We have not found that selection across generations works. We have not found anything about frontier models; everything here is 8B and text. We have not found the framing's sources to be anything but sources. Anyone who reads this piece as a result on the hypothesis has misread it, and the formal report says the same in the lab's words.

Where it sits

The two benchmarks we use were built to measure exactly this kind of behaviour in text worlds: Jiminy Cricket (Hendrycks and colleagues, 2021) and MACHIAVELLI (Pan and colleagues, 2023). Both papers report agents trained with and without a moral signal; the first paper is not a replication of those experiments, and the plan's later revision may add that comparison as one of its checks. The plan's watcher problem, whether an agent behaves differently when it thinks it is scored, is the subject of a run of recent frontier-scale work: alignment faking (Greenblatt and colleagues, 2024), in-context scheming (Meinke and colleagues, 2024), and models knowing when they are evaluated (Needham and colleagues, 2025); whether any of it shows up at 8B is one of our measurements, not an assumption. The agents we will build with hidden faults, to test whether our detectors work, build on the sleeper-agents work (Hubinger and colleagues, 2024) and the probe results that followed it. The idea of guiding an agent through a channel it cannot tell from chance builds on Anthropic's introspection experiments (Lindsey and colleagues, a post in October 2025 and a preprint in January 2026) and on three later studies, listed in the prior-work table, that ran the same kind of test on open models. And the generational idea's nearest verified relative is a study of the cultural evolution of cooperation among language-model agents (Vallinder and Hughes, 2024), which has the generations and an endpoint but not the developmental recipe, the blind copy rule, the robustness suite or the preregistration. The full list, eighty-nine arXiv papers checked by fetch, five web sources read and three more to check, is on the branch.

Eight words, briefly

8B: the model's size, eight billion parameters, small by today's standards. Open weights: a model whose parameters anyone can download and run. Seed: the number that sets a run's random choices; different seeds, different runs. Null: a result that finds no effect. Sealed test set: games kept unread until the final measurement; today the list sits in a plaintext file in a private repository, hashed and not read into any training or selection, and before the first paper trains it passes to someone who is not me. Positive control: a known method used to prove the instrument can see a difference. Kill rule: a rule written in advance that stops the grant ask if the instrument's wobble is above a stated limit. Preregistered plan: the tests and their bars written down and hashed before any training run, so they cannot be bent to fit a result; ours is frozen and hashed today and will be publicly registered before the first paper trains. The paper's glossary has the rest.

What would change this piece

The eight-seed number and the goal-tracking floor are in. Next: the positive control moving, or not; the persistent world's first check; and then the first paper. Only the first paper can rewrite the headline; the others sharpen the instrument it rests on. Until then the headline is the honest one at the top.
